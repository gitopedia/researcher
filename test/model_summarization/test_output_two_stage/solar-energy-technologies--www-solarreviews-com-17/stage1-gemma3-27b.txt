# Solar Energy History and Development

**Early Discoveries**

- In 1839, Alexandre Edmond Becquerel discovered the photovoltaic effect while experimenting with metal electrodes and acidic solutions, creating the first solar cell capable of carrying an electric current from light.
- Charles Fritts created the first functioning solar panels in 1883, using selenium wafers, though they were inefficient with only a 1% energy conversion rate.
- Edward Weston received the first U.S. patent for a solar cell in 1888, furthering research into more efficient solar panels.
- Nikola Tesla received a U.S. patent in 1901 for a method of utilizing radiant energy, essentially a patent for solar panels.
- Albert Einstein published a paper in 1905 on the photoelectric effect, providing the theoretical basis for how solar cells create energy and later earning him the Nobel Prize in 1922.

**Mid-20th Century Advancements**

- In 1954, Gerald Pearson, Daryl Chapin, and Calvin Fuller at Bell Labs created the first high-power silicon solar photovoltaic (PV) cell, improving efficiency by using silicon instead of selenium.
- Sharp Corporation began mass production of silicon solar panels in 1963, following the creation of a viable PV module. Japan installed a 242-watt PV array on a lighthouse, the largest of its time.
- NASA launched the first Nimbus satellite with a 470-watt PV array in 1964, following the launch of Vanguard I by the Naval Research Laboratory.
- The 1970s saw a surge in solar research, driving down PV costs by 80% and allowing for testing and adoption of solar for off-grid applications.
- The University of Delaware built "Solar One" in 1973, one of the world’s first PV-powered buildings, utilizing both PV panels and solar thermal energy.
- Kyocera began production of thin-film silicon ribbon crystal solar modules in 1976, simplifying the manufacturing process.
- The U.S. Department of Energy established the U.S. Solar Energy Research Institute (now NREL) in 1977, dedicated to renewable energy research and energy independence.

**Late 20th and Early 21st Century Growth**

- Germany launched the "100,000 Solar Roofs" program in 1999, supporting the installation of PV systems larger than 1 kilowatt.
- The Energy Policy Act of 2005 created a 30% investment tax credit (ITC) for commercial and residential solar energy systems, which has been extended and revamped several times.
- The California Public Utilities Commission launched the California Solar Initiative (CSI) in 2006, a $3 billion program for solar incentive programs in California.
- By 2007, solar energy became the leading clean energy technology for venture capital and private equity investment, with global investment in clean energy exceeding $100 billion.
- From 2008 to 2012, the global cost of solar modules fell dramatically from approximately $5 per watt to $1 per watt, driven by subsidies in Germany and new programs in other countries.
- From 2012 to 2015, residential solar system installations became cost-effective for many American households, with prices falling from $6.62 per watt to $3.36 per watt.

**Recent Developments and Current Status**

- In 2015, Tesla announced the launch of the Powerwall, a lithium-ion storage product for homeowners, offering a low-cost solution for storing solar power.
- The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) passed in 2022 extended the federal solar tax credit until 2034 and provided billions in funding for state energy offices and domestic solar manufacturing.
- As of 2023, the average cost of a residential solar system ranges from $16,500 to $21,000 before incentives.
- California currently leads the U.S. solar market with over 46,874 Megawatts (MW) of solar installed.
- Over 92% of residential solar installers now offer energy storage installations.
- Some of the best states for solar installations include Massachusetts, Colorado, Washington D.C., and Maryland. 
- Financing options for solar include loans, leases, and power-purchase agreements.
