# Climate Change

- Climate change refers to significant and lasting changes in the Earth's climate, particularly an increase in global temperatures over time.
- It is primarily driven by human activities, especially the emission of greenhouse gases like carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), and nitrous oxide (N₂O).
- The most significant source of greenhouse gas emissions is the burning of fossil fuels for energy, transportation, and industrial processes.
- Deforestation and changes in land use also contribute to climate change by reducing the planet's ability to absorb CO₂ from the atmosphere.
- The Earth's average surface temperature has risen by approximately 1.1°C (2°F) since the late 19th century, with most of the warming occurring in the past 40 years.
- The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) is the leading international body for the assessment of climate change, providing scientific reports that guide global policy.
- The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report (2021-2023) confirmed that human influence is unequivocally the main cause of observed warming since the mid-20th century.
- Climate change is not limited to rising temperatures; it also leads to changes in precipitation patterns, more frequent and intense weather events, and shifts in ecosystems.
- The greenhouse effect is a natural process that warms the Earth's surface, but human activities have intensified this effect, leading to global warming.
- Greenhouse gases trap heat in the atmosphere, creating a "blanket" effect that prevents heat from escaping into space.
- Carbon dioxide is the most prevalent greenhouse gas emitted by human activities, with levels in the atmosphere reaching over 420 parts per million (ppm) in 2023, the highest in at least 800,000 years.
- Methane is a more potent greenhouse gas than CO₂, with a global warming potential (GWP) 28–36 times higher over a 100-year period.
- Methane emissions come from sources such as livestock farming, rice paddies, and the production and transport of coal, oil, and natural gas.
- Nitrous oxide, another powerful greenhouse gas, is primarily emitted from agricultural and industrial activities, as well as the combustion of fossil fuels and solid waste.
- Fluorinated gases are synthetic gases with high global warming potential, used in refrigeration, air conditioning, and industrial applications.
- The Earth's climate system includes the atmosphere, hydrosphere (oceans, lakes, rivers), cryosphere (ice and snow), biosphere (living organisms), and lithosphere (land surfaces).
- Climate change affects all components of the Earth's system, leading to feedback loops that can amplify or dampen the initial warming.
- One of the most well-known feedback loops is the albedo effect, where melting ice reduces the Earth's reflectivity, causing more heat to be absorbed and further warming.
- The Arctic is warming at more than twice the global average rate, leading to rapid ice loss, permafrost thaw, and changes in ecosystems.
- Permafrost, which contains vast amounts of organic carbon, is thawing due to rising temperatures, releasing CO₂ and methane into the atmosphere and accelerating warming.
- Oceans absorb about 90% of the excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases, leading to ocean warming and thermal expansion, which contributes to sea level rise.
- The oceans also absorb about a quarter of the CO₂ emitted by human activities, leading to ocean acidification, which harms marine life, especially organisms with calcium carbonate shells or skeletons.
- Sea level rise is occurring at an accelerating rate due to thermal expansion of seawater and the melting of glaciers and ice sheets, particularly in Greenland and Antarctica.
- Coastal communities are especially vulnerable to sea level rise, which can lead to flooding, erosion, saltwater intrusion into freshwater supplies, and displacement of populations.
- Climate change is increasing the frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, including heatwaves, droughts, heavy rainfall, hurricanes, and wildfires.
- Heatwaves are becoming more frequent and severe, posing risks to human health, agriculture, and energy systems.
- Droughts are intensifying in many regions, leading to water shortages, reduced crop yields, and increased risk of wildfires.
- Heavy rainfall and flooding are becoming more common in some areas, overwhelming infrastructure and causing damage to homes, businesses, and ecosystems.
- Hurricanes and tropical storms are becoming more intense due to warmer ocean temperatures, with higher wind speeds and greater rainfall.
- Wildfires are increasing in frequency and severity, particularly in regions with dry climates and prolonged droughts, such as the western United States, Australia, and the Mediterranean.
- Climate change is affecting biodiversity, with many species struggling to adapt to rapidly changing conditions, leading to shifts in habitats, migration patterns, and extinction risks.
- Some species are moving toward the poles or to higher elevations in response to warming temperatures, but not all can migrate or adapt quickly enough.
- Coral reefs are particularly vulnerable to climate change, with rising ocean temperatures and acidification causing widespread coral bleaching and death.
- The Great Barrier Reef in Australia has experienced multiple mass bleaching events in recent years, with significant portions of the reef dying or becoming degraded.
- Climate change is also affecting food security, as changes in temperature and precipitation patterns disrupt agricultural productivity and crop yields.
- Staple crops such as wheat, rice, and maize are sensitive to temperature changes, with higher temperatures generally reducing yields.
- In some regions, climate change may lead to longer growing seasons, but in many others, it will result in shorter and less predictable growing periods.
- Climate change is expected to increase the spread of vector-borne diseases, as warmer temperatures expand the range of disease-carrying organisms such as mosquitoes and ticks.
- Diseases like malaria, dengue fever, and Lyme disease are becoming more prevalent in new regions due to climate change.
- The health impacts of climate change include heat-related illnesses, respiratory problems from increased air pollution, and the spread of infectious diseases.
- Vulnerable populations, such as the elderly, children, and those with pre-existing health conditions, are at greater risk from the health effects of climate change.
- Climate change is a global issue that requires international cooperation to address, as the effects transcend national borders and impact all countries.
- The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) was established in 1992 to address climate change through international collaboration.
- The Kyoto Protocol, adopted in 1997, was the first international agreement to set binding emission reduction targets for developed countries.
- The Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015, is a landmark international treaty that aims to limit global warming to well below 2°C, preferably to 1.5°C, compared to pre-industrial levels.
- Under the Paris Agreement, countries submit nationally determined contributions (NDCs) outlining their climate action plans and emission reduction targets.
- The goal of limiting warming to 1.5°C is considered critical to avoiding the most severe impacts of climate change, as even 2°C of warming could lead to catastrophic consequences.
- Achieving the goals of the Paris Agreement requires a rapid and large-scale transformation of energy systems, transportation, agriculture, and industry.
- Renewable energy sources such as solar, wind, and hydropower are essential for reducing greenhouse gas emissions and transitioning to a low-carbon economy.
- The transition to renewable energy is already underway, with global renewable energy capacity increasing significantly in recent years.
- Solar and wind energy are the fastest-growing sources of electricity generation, with costs declining rapidly, making them increasingly competitive with fossil fuels.
- Energy efficiency improvements in buildings, transportation, and industry are also critical for reducing emissions and conserving resources.
- Electric vehicles (EVs) are becoming more popular as a cleaner alternative to internal combustion engine vehicles, although the environmental benefits depend on the source of electricity used to charge them.
- Sustainable agriculture practices, such as reducing food waste, improving soil health, and reducing the use of synthetic fertilizers, can help reduce emissions from the agricultural sector.
- Reducing deforestation and promoting reforestation and afforestation are important strategies for removing CO₂ from the atmosphere and preserving biodiversity.
- Carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies aim to capture CO₂ emissions from industrial processes and store them underground to prevent them from entering the atmosphere.
- Carbon pricing mechanisms, such as carbon taxes and cap-and-trade systems, are policy tools designed to incentivize the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions.
- International financial support is needed to help developing countries transition to low-carbon economies and adapt to the impacts of climate change.
- Climate finance is a key component of the Paris Agreement, with developed countries committing to mobilize $100 billion per year by 2020 to support climate action in developing countries.
- Adaptation strategies are necessary to help communities and ecosystems cope with the unavoidable impacts of climate change, such as sea level rise and extreme weather events.
- Adaptation measures include building resilient infrastructure, improving water management, and developing early warning systems for extreme weather events.
- Climate justice is an important aspect of the global response to climate change, as the impacts are often felt most severely by the poorest and most vulnerable populations, despite their minimal contribution to emissions.
- Indigenous communities and small island nations are particularly vulnerable to climate change and often lack the resources to adapt effectively.
- Public awareness and education about climate change are essential for building support for climate action and encouraging individual and collective efforts to reduce emissions.
- Individual actions, such as reducing energy consumption, eating less meat, and using public transportation, can contribute to reducing greenhouse gas emissions.
- Governments, businesses, and civil society all have roles to play in addressing climate change through policy, innovation, and advocacy.
- Climate change is one of the most pressing challenges of the 21st century, requiring urgent and sustained action at all levels to mitigate its impacts and build a sustainable future.
